In the early 1960s, the emergence of the theory of plate tectonics started a revolution in the earth sciences. Since then, scientists have verified and refined this theory, and now have a much better understanding of how our planet has been shaped by plate-tectonic processes. We now know that, directly or indirectly, plate tectonics influences nearly all geologic processes, past and present. Indeed, the notion that the entire Earth's surface is continually shifting has profoundly changed the way we view our world.
People benefit from, and are at the mercy of, the forces and consequences of plate tectonics. With little or no warning, an earthquake or volcanic eruption can unleash bursts of energy far more powerful than anything we can generate. While we have no control over plate-tectonic processes, we now have the knowledge to learn from them. The more we know about plate tectonics, the better we can appreciate the grandeur and beauty of the land upon which we live, as well as the occasional violent displays of the Earth's awesome power.
Plate tectonics provides a broad framework for understanding how Earth's surface changes over time. The development of the theory drew on many discoveries and the work of numerous scientists, and its history shows how scientific ideas are tested, refined, and strengthened. Although the general idea of plate tectonics is now widely accepted, many details still challenge researchers and continue to inspire new investigation. In that sense, the earth-science revolution launched by plate tectonics is still unfolding.
In geologic terms, a plate is a large, rigid slab of solid rock. The word tectonics comes from a Greek root meaning "to build." Together, the term plate tectonics refers to the way Earth's surface is constructed from moving plates. The theory of plate tectonics states that Earth's outermost layer is broken into a dozen or more large and small plates that move relative to one another as they ride atop hotter, more mobile material below.
Even before plate tectonics became widely accepted, some scientists had proposed that today's continents were once joined as parts of larger landmasses, often called supercontinents. One of the most important examples is Pangaea, a name meaning "all lands" in Greek. The idea that Pangaea later broke apart figured prominently in the theory of continental drift, which served as an important forerunner to modern plate tectonics.
According to the theory of continental drift, the supercontinent Pangaea began to break apart about 225 to 200 million years ago, eventually fragmenting into the continents we know today.
Plate tectonics is a relatively recent scientific concept, yet it has transformed our understanding of the dynamic planet on which we live. The theory has unified many branches of the earth sciences, from paleontology to seismology, and has helped explain why earthquakes and volcanic eruptions cluster in particular regions and how major mountain ranges such as the Alps and Himalayas formed.
Questions about why Earth is so restless have been asked for centuries. Why does the ground shake violently, why do volcanoes erupt with explosive force, and why do great mountain ranges rise to such heights? Until the 1700s, many Europeans believed that a Biblical Flood played a major role in shaping Earth's surface. This view, known as catastrophism, treated earthly change as the product of sudden destructive events. By the mid-19th century, however, catastrophism gave way to uniformitarianism, a framework associated with James Hutton's idea that "the present is the key to the past." In this view, the geologic forces and processes acting on Earth today, whether gradual or catastrophic, are fundamentally the same kinds of processes that operated in the geologic past.
The idea that continents have not always remained fixed in their present positions was proposed long before the 20th century. As early as 1596, the Dutch map maker Abraham Ortelius suggested that the Americas had been torn away from Europe and Africa. The idea resurfaced in the 19th century, but it was not until 1912 that Alfred Lothar Wegener presented Continental Drift as a full scientific theory. Wegener argued that about 200 million years ago the supercontinent Pangaea began to split apart. Later, Alexander Du Toit proposed that Pangaea first separated into two major landmasses, Laurasia in the north and Gondwanaland in the south, which then broke into the smaller continents known today.
In 1858, geographer Antonio Snider-Pellegrini had already published maps showing how the American and African continents might once have fit together before later separating. Such reconstructions provided visual support for the idea that continents had moved over geologic time.
Wegener's theory was based in part on what appeared to him to be the remarkable fit of the South American and African continents, first noted by Abraham Ortelius three centuries earlier. Wegener was also intrigued by the occurrences of unusual geologic structures and of plant and animal fossils found on the matching coastlines of South America and Africa, which are now widely separated by the Atlantic Ocean. He reasoned that it was physically impossible for most of these organisms to have swum or have been transported across the vast oceans. To him, the presence of identical fossil species along the coastal parts of Africa and South America was the most compelling evidence that the two continents were once joined.
In Wegener's mind, the drifting of continents after the break-up of Pangaea explained not only the matching fossil occurrences but also the evidence of dramatic climate changes on some continents. For example, the discovery of fossils of tropical plants (in the form of coal deposits) in Antarctica led to the conclusion that this frozen land previously must have been situated closer to the equator, in a more temperate climate where lush, swampy vegetation could grow. Other mismatches of geology and climate included distinctive fossil ferns (Glossopteris) discovered in now-polar regions, and the occurrence of glacial deposits in present-day arid Africa, such as the Vaal River valley of South Africa.
The theory of continental drift would become the spark that ignited a new way of viewing the Earth. But at the time Wegener introduced his theory, the scientific community firmly believed the continents and oceans to be permanent features on the Earth's surface. Not surprisingly, his proposal was not well received, even though it seemed to agree with the scientific information available at the time. A fatal weakness in Wegener's theory was that it could not satisfactorily answer the most fundamental question raised by his critics: What kind of forces could be strong enough to move such large masses of solid rock over such great distances? Wegener suggested that the continents simply plowed through the ocean floor, but Harold Jeffreys, a noted English geophysicist, argued correctly that it was physically impossible for a large mass of solid rock to plow through the ocean floor without breaking up.
Undaunted by rejection, Wegener devoted the rest of his life to doggedly pursuing additional evidence to defend his theory. He froze to death in 1930 during an expedition crossing the Greenland ice cap, but the controversy he spawned raged on. However, after his death, new evidence from ocean floor exploration and other studies rekindled interest in Wegener's theory, ultimately leading to the development of the theory of plate tectonics.
Plate tectonics has proven to be as important to the earth sciences as the discovery of the structure of the atom was to physics and chemistry and the theory of evolution was to the life sciences. Even though the theory of plate tectonics is now widely accepted by the scientific community, aspects of the theory are still being debated today. Ironically, one of the chief outstanding questions is the one Wegener failed to resolve: What is the nature of the forces propelling the plates? Scientists also debate how plate tectonics may have operated (if at all) earlier in the Earth's history and whether similar processes operate, or have ever operated, on other planets in our solar system.
Continental drift remained controversial for decades after Wegener's death, and many scientists dismissed it as implausible. Beginning in the 1950s, however, a large body of new evidence revived the debate and helped transform continental drift into the broader theory of plate tectonics. Four developments were especially important: detailed mapping of the ocean floor, recognition of repeated reversals of Earth's magnetic field, the seafloor-spreading hypothesis and the recycling of oceanic crust, and precise evidence that earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated in specific global belts.
About two thirds of Earth's surface lies beneath the oceans, yet for a long time many people assumed the seafloor was flat and featureless. As measurements improved, especially through bathymetric surveys and later echo sounding, scientists discovered that the ocean floor is rugged and dynamic. These studies revealed vast submarine mountain chains, including the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and showed that the mid-ocean ridge system forms a continuous global feature more than 50,000 kilometers long. The thinness of sediment on the ocean floor also suggested that much of the seafloor is geologically young rather than ancient and unchanged.
Another breakthrough came from magnetic measurements of oceanic basalt. Because basalt contains magnetite, it can preserve the direction of Earth's magnetic field at the time the rock cooled. Scientists found that the seafloor contains alternating bands of normal and reversed magnetic polarity arranged in symmetrical stripes on both sides of mid-ocean ridges. This pattern showed that Earth's magnetic field had reversed many times in the past and that new oceanic crust was forming at the ridges and moving outward over time.
These observations led to the seafloor-spreading hypothesis. Scientists proposed that mid-ocean ridges are places where magma rises from below, cools, and creates new oceanic crust. As new crust forms, older crust is pushed away from the ridge crest. Evidence supported this interpretation: rocks nearest the ridge are youngest, rocks farther away are older, and their magnetic patterns record repeated polarity reversals in an orderly way. Later drilling of deep-ocean cores provided strong confirmation, showing that oceanic crust becomes progressively older with distance from the ridge.
Seafloor spreading raised an important question: if new crust is constantly being created, why does Earth not keep growing larger? The answer is that old oceanic crust is also destroyed. Harry Hess and Robert Dietz argued that as crust moves away from spreading ridges, it eventually sinks into deep ocean trenches and returns to the mantle. In this view, ocean basins are continuously recycled. New lithosphere forms at ridges while older lithosphere is consumed at subduction zones, allowing Earth's overall size to remain essentially unchanged.
Earthquake research provided another major line of evidence. With improved seismographs and global seismic networks, scientists found that earthquakes are not distributed randomly. Instead, they cluster along mid-ocean ridges, deep-sea trenches, and other plate boundaries. In some trench regions, earthquake foci define inclined zones descending deep into the Earth, now called Wadati-Benioff zones. These patterns matched the idea that new crust is generated at ridges and older lithosphere sinks back into the mantle at trenches.
Together, ocean-floor mapping, magnetic striping, seafloor spreading, and global earthquake patterns gave scientists a coherent explanation for continental motion and many other geologic processes. Plate tectonics emerged not from one observation alone but from the convergence of many independent discoveries. This is one reason the theory became so powerful: it connected mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes, continents, and ocean basins within a single dynamic picture of Earth.
Continental drift was debated for decades after Wegener's death and was often dismissed as improbable. Beginning in the 1950s, however, a large amount of new evidence revived interest in his ideas. Four major developments were especially important: the discovery that the ocean floor is rugged and geologically young, the confirmation that Earth's magnetic field has reversed many times in the past, the emergence of the seafloor-spreading hypothesis and the recycling of oceanic crust, and the precise documentation that earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated along oceanic trenches and submarine mountain ranges.
Ocean floor mapping provided one of the first key advances. About two thirds of Earth's surface lies beneath the oceans, yet for a long time many people assumed the seafloor was relatively flat and featureless. Early depth measurements showed that the open ocean varied greatly in depth, and later exploration greatly improved scientific knowledge of the ocean floor. Scientists came to understand that many geologic processes on land are linked directly or indirectly to the dynamics of the ocean floor.
Modern measurements of ocean depth increased in the nineteenth century, when bathymetric surveys were routinely conducted in the Atlantic and Caribbean. In 1855, a chart published by Matthew Maury provided the first evidence of underwater mountains in the central Atlantic. This finding was later confirmed by survey ships involved in laying the trans-Atlantic telegraph cable. After World War I, echo-sounding devices began to measure ocean depth by timing how long sound signals took to travel from a ship to the seafloor and back. These measurements showed that the ocean floor was much more rugged than previously believed and demonstrated the continuity of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
In 1947, seismologists on the research ship Atlantis found that the sediment layer on the floor of the Atlantic Ocean was much thinner than expected. Scientists had assumed that the oceans were extremely old and that the seafloor should therefore be covered by a thick layer of sediment. The unexpected thinness of the sediment later became an important clue in the development of plate tectonics.
During the 1950s, oceanographic surveys by many nations revealed that a great mountain system on the seafloor nearly encircles the Earth. This global mid-ocean ridge extends for more than 50,000 kilometers and in some places is more than 800 kilometers wide. Although hidden beneath the ocean surface, it is the most prominent topographic feature on the surface of the planet.
Magnetic striping and polar reversals provided another major line of evidence. Beginning in the 1950s, scientists used magnetometers to detect magnetic variations across the ocean floor. Basalt, the volcanic rock that makes up much of the ocean floor, contains magnetite and can preserve magnetic information. Because of this property, magnetic measurements became a powerful tool for studying the deep ocean floor.
Earlier paleomagnetic studies had already shown that rocks can record either normal polarity or reversed polarity relative to Earth's present magnetic field. Magnetite grains within cooling volcanic rock align with the direction of the magnetic field at the time the rock solidifies. Once the rock cools, this magnetic orientation is locked in, preserving a record of the field's polarity.
As scientists mapped more of the seafloor, they found that magnetic variations were not random. Instead, the ocean floor displayed a zebra-like pattern of alternating bands of normally and reversely magnetized rock on both sides of the mid-ocean ridge. This pattern became known as magnetic striping.
The discovery of magnetic striping led directly to the seafloor-spreading hypothesis. In 1961, scientists proposed that mid-ocean ridges are weak zones where the ocean floor is pulled apart along the ridge crest. New magma rises from deep within the Earth through these weak zones and erupts to form new oceanic crust. Over millions of years, this process builds the global system of mid-ocean ridges.
Several observations supported the seafloor-spreading hypothesis. Rocks near the crest of a ridge are very young, and rocks become progressively older farther from the crest. The youngest rocks near the ridge have normal magnetic polarity, and bands of rock parallel to the ridge alternate in polarity. Together, these observations suggested that Earth's magnetic field has reversed many times and that new crust is created at ridges and carried outward over time. By explaining both the magnetic striping pattern and the formation of the ridge system, seafloor spreading became a major advance in plate-tectonic theory. Oceanic crust came to be understood as a natural record of magnetic reversals in Earth's history.
Additional evidence came from deep-ocean drilling. In 1968, the research vessel Glomar Challenger collected drill-core samples from the Atlantic Ocean on both sides of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Paleontological and isotopic dating of these samples showed that the age of the oceanic crust increases with distance from the ridge, providing strong confirmation of seafloor spreading.
Seafloor spreading had an important implication: new crust is continually formed along oceanic ridges. Some scientists suggested that this meant the Earth itself must be expanding, but the expanding-Earth hypothesis lacked a convincing geologic mechanism. Most geologists concluded instead that Earth has changed little in overall size since its formation, raising a key question: how can new crust be added without making the planet larger?
Harry Hess and Robert Dietz proposed the answer. If new crust forms at oceanic ridges, old crust must be destroyed elsewhere. Hess suggested that oceanic crust moves away from ridges like a conveyor belt and eventually sinks into deep ocean trenches. In this view, the Atlantic Ocean was expanding while the Pacific Ocean was shrinking. New crust forms at spreading ridges, while old oceanic lithosphere is consumed at trenches. This process recycles the ocean basins and explains why Earth does not increase in size, why there is relatively little sediment on the ocean floor, and why oceanic rocks are much younger than continental rocks.
The concentration of earthquakes supplied another major line of evidence. During the twentieth century, improvements in seismic instruments and the increasing use of seismographs around the world showed that earthquakes are concentrated in particular regions, especially along oceanic trenches and spreading ridges. By the late 1920s, seismologists had identified major earthquake zones parallel to trenches that dip into the Earth at angles of about forty to sixty degrees and extend several hundred kilometers downward. These became known as Wadati-Benioff zones.
Global seismic studies improved dramatically in the 1960s with the establishment of the Worldwide Standardized Seismograph Network. The much better data allowed scientists to map earthquake zones with high precision. These patterns showed a strong connection between earthquakes and major seafloor features.
The relationship between earthquakes, trenches, and ridges helped confirm the seafloor-spreading hypothesis. Earthquakes mark the zones where oceanic crust is created along ridges and the zones where oceanic lithosphere sinks back into the mantle beneath trenches. Together with ocean-floor mapping, magnetic striping, and drilling results, this evidence helped establish the modern theory of plate tectonics.
Scientists now have a fairly good understanding of how the plates move and how such movements relate to earthquake activity. Most movement occurs along narrow zones between plates where the results of plate-tectonic forces are most evident.
There are four types of plate boundaries. Divergent boundaries are places where new crust is generated as plates pull away from each other. Convergent boundaries are places where crust is destroyed as one plate dives under another. Transform boundaries are places where crust is neither produced nor destroyed as plates slide horizontally past each other. Plate boundary zones are broad belts where boundaries are not well defined and the effects of plate interaction are unclear.
Divergent boundaries occur along spreading centers where plates move apart and new crust is created by magma rising from the mantle. A well-known example is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a submerged mountain range that forms one segment of the global mid-ocean ridge system. The spreading rate along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge averages about 2.5 centimeters per year. Although that rate seems slow, over millions of years it has produced plate movement of thousands of kilometers and has caused the Atlantic Ocean to grow from a small body of water into a vast ocean.
Iceland provides a natural laboratory for studying divergent boundaries on land because it straddles the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Iceland is splitting along the spreading center between the North American Plate and the Eurasian Plate, as North America moves westward relative to Eurasia. Around Krafla Volcano in northeastern Iceland, existing cracks have widened and new cracks have appeared repeatedly. Between 1975 and 1984, many rifting episodes occurred there, some accompanied by volcanic activity. During that period, the total displacement caused by rifting was about seven meters.
In East Africa, spreading processes have already torn Saudi Arabia away from the rest of Africa, forming the Red Sea. The African Plate and the Arabian Plate meet at a triple junction where the Red Sea meets the Gulf of Aden. A new spreading center may be developing under Africa along the East African Rift Zone. When continental crust stretches beyond its limit, tension cracks form, magma rises through them, and volcanoes may develop. If spreading continues, the Horn of Africa may eventually separate from the rest of the continent and become a large island.
Convergent boundaries are places where plates move toward each other and crust is recycled. Because Earth's size has not changed significantly over geologic time, crust must be destroyed at about the same rate at which it is created. This destruction takes place where one plate is subducted beneath another at a subduction zone. The exact form of convergence depends on the kinds of lithosphere involved: oceanic-continental convergence, oceanic-oceanic convergence, or continental-continental convergence.
At oceanic-continental convergent boundaries, an oceanic plate is subducted beneath a continental plate. Trenches mark these zones and are among the deepest parts of the ocean floor. Along the Peru-Chile Trench, the oceanic Nazca Plate is being subducted beneath the South American Plate. This process has helped create the Andes Mountains and produces strong earthquakes and rapid uplift. Large subduction earthquakes may occur when parts of the descending plate become locked for long periods before suddenly moving.
Oceanic-continental convergence also supports many active volcanoes, such as those in the Andes and the Cascade Range. The volcanic activity is closely associated with subduction, although scientists still debate whether the magma comes mainly from the descending slab, the overlying continental lithosphere, or both.
At oceanic-oceanic convergent boundaries, one oceanic plate is usually subducted beneath the other. This process forms deep trenches such as the Marianas Trench, where the Pacific Plate converges with the Philippine Plate. Subduction also creates chains of volcanic islands called island arcs. Over long periods, erupted lava and volcanic debris build submarine volcanoes upward until they rise above sea level. Island arcs such as the Marianas and the Aleutian Islands are closely associated with trenches and frequent strong earthquakes.
At continental-continental convergent boundaries, neither plate is easily subducted because continental rocks are relatively light. Instead, the crust buckles, thickens, and is pushed upward. The Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau formed when India collided with Asia about 50 million years ago. Continued convergence over millions of years uplifted the region to extreme elevations. The Himalayas are the highest continental mountains on Earth, and the Tibetan Plateau is one of the highest and broadest elevated regions in the world.
Transform boundaries are zones where two plates slide horizontally past one another. These are also called transform-fault boundaries. Most transform faults are found on the ocean floor, where they offset segments of mid-ocean ridges and are often marked by shallow earthquakes. Some transform faults occur on land. A famous example is the San Andreas fault zone in California, which links spreading centers to the south and north and represents horizontal motion between major plates.
Most earthquakes and volcanic eruptions occur near plate boundaries, but there are important exceptions. The Hawaiian Islands, for example, are entirely volcanic in origin and formed in the middle of the Pacific Ocean far from the nearest plate boundary. Such examples led scientists to ask how volcanoes that form within plate interiors fit into plate tectonics.
In 1963, J. Tuzo Wilson proposed what became known as the hotspot theory. He suggested that some long-lived volcanic regions are caused by relatively small and exceptionally hot areas beneath tectonic plates. These hotspots provide localized sources of heat that can generate magma and sustain volcanism for very long periods of time.
Wilson used the Hawaiian Island-Emperor seamount chain as a key example. He proposed that the Pacific Plate moves over a deep, nearly stationary hotspot in the mantle beneath the present-day Island of Hawaii. Heat from the hotspot partly melts the overlying Pacific Plate, producing magma that rises through the crust and erupts on the seafloor. Repeated eruptions build a seamount upward until it rises above sea level to form a volcanic island. As plate motion carries the island away from the hotspot, the magma supply is cut off and volcanism stops. A new volcano then begins forming above the hotspot, and over millions of years this process creates a long chain of islands and seamounts.
According to hotspot theory, volcanoes in the Hawaiian chain should become progressively older and more eroded with increasing distance from the active hotspot. This pattern is observed in Hawaii. On Kauai, the northwesternmost of the main Hawaiian Islands, the oldest volcanic rocks are about 5.5 million years old and are deeply eroded. On the Big Island of Hawaii, which lies above the active hotspot, the oldest exposed rocks are less than 0.7 million years old and new volcanic rock is still being formed.
Long before modern geological studies, ancient Hawaiians had already recognized that the islands become younger toward the southeast. Through observations of erosion, soil formation, and vegetation, they understood that islands such as Niihau and Kauai were older than Maui and Hawaii. This idea was preserved in the legends of Pele, the Goddess of Volcanoes, whose movement from older islands to younger ones reflects the geological pattern of volcanic island growth and later erosion.
Although Hawaii is the best known example, many other hotspots are thought to exist beneath both oceans and continents. More than one hundred hotspots have likely been active during the past ten million years. Most lie within plate interiors, though some occur near divergent boundaries or close to the mid-ocean ridge system, such as beneath Iceland, the Azores, and the Galapagos Islands.
A well-known continental example is the hotspot thought to exist beneath Yellowstone National Park in northwestern Wyoming. This region contains several calderas formed by three enormous eruptions during the past two million years, the most recent about 600,000 years ago. Ash from these eruptions has been found far from Yellowstone, including in Iowa, Missouri, Texas, and northern Mexico.
The thermal energy associated with the Yellowstone hotspot drives more than ten thousand hydrothermal features, including hot springs, geysers, and mudpots. A large body of magma still exists beneath the caldera, capped by a hydrothermal system of pressurized hot water and steam. Modern measurements show that parts of the Yellowstone region rise and fall by as much as one centimeter per year, indicating that the area remains geologically active. However, these ground movements most likely reflect changes in hydrothermal pressure and do not necessarily mean that a volcanic eruption is imminent.
Later scientific work raised questions about the classic plume interpretation of hotspots. Some studies suggest that hotspots may not always be deep in origin or fixed in position over geologic time. Even so, hotspot volcanism remains an important idea for explaining why some volcanic regions form far from plate boundaries.
Tectonic plates do not drift randomly across Earth's surface. Their motion is driven by real but not directly visible forces. Although scientists do not yet fully understand these forces, most think that relatively shallow forces acting on the lithosphere are connected to processes occurring much deeper inside Earth.
Many geologists accept Harry Hess's idea that plate motion is driven by the slow movement of hot, softened mantle beneath the rigid plates. This idea was already considered in the 1930s by Arthur Holmes, who suggested that circular motion in the mantle could carry continents in a conveyor-belt-like way. At the time of Wegener's continental drift theory, many scientists still thought of Earth as a solid and motionless body. We now know that both Earth's surface and interior are mobile. Beneath the lithospheric plates, the mantle can flow slowly under heat and pressure over very long periods of time.
The mobile rock beneath the rigid plates is often compared to thick soup circulating in a heated pot. Hot material rises, spreads, cools, and sinks again, creating what scientists call convection cells or convective flow. Although this analogy is useful, mantle convection is much slower and far more complex than boiling liquid. Many questions remain unanswered, including how many convection cells exist, where they originate, and what their full structure is.
Convection requires a source of heat. Earth's internal heat comes mainly from two sources: radioactive decay and residual heat from planetary formation. Radioactive decay of elements such as uranium, thorium, and potassium releases heat that slowly moves toward the surface. Residual heat is the gravitational energy left over from the formation of Earth about 4.6 billion years ago, when cosmic material came together and was compressed. Scientists still do not fully understand how this internal heat becomes concentrated in specific regions to produce convection cells.
Until the 1990s, many explanations of plate motion emphasized mantle convection and regarded seafloor spreading as the main driving mechanism. In this view, colder and denser material sinks while hotter and lighter material rises, and magma intruding at spreading ridges may also help push plates apart through a process called ridge push. Subduction was then seen mainly as a secondary consequence of seafloor spreading.
More recently, many scientists have come to favor the idea that subduction-related forces are more important than seafloor spreading in driving plate tectonics. In particular, slab pull is now widely considered a major driving force. Slab pull occurs when a cold, dense oceanic plate sinks into a subduction zone under gravity and drags the rest of the plate along with it.
Scientists know that forces deep inside Earth drive plate motion, but the details remain uncertain. None of the proposed mechanisms explains every aspect of plate movement, and because these processes occur so deep below the surface, they cannot be tested directly. The fact that plates moved in the past and still move today is clear, but the exact reasons and mechanisms will likely remain an active scientific question for a long time.
Earth may be unique in the solar system because it appears to be the only planet that is still both volcanically and tectonically active. Plate tectonics depends on the escape of internal heat, which also fuels volcanism. Smaller bodies such as the Moon, Mars, and probably Mercury lost their internal heat more quickly than Earth and have been largely inactive for a very long time.
Venus may still be active, although the evidence is uncertain. Measurements from the Pioneer-Venus spacecraft detected a large amount of sulfur in the atmosphere in 1979, which later declined and may have reflected a volcanic event. Radar images from the Magellan spacecraft also revealed volcanic features and long deep valleys that resemble trenches on Earth.
Io, one of Jupiter's moons, is highly volcanic and shows plumes rising far above its surface. Scientists think this activity may be powered by tidal heating caused by the gravitational interaction between Io and Jupiter. That heating may be enough to generate convection inside Io, although scientists have not clearly identified surface features that prove plate tectonics there.
Ganymede, another moon of Jupiter, has a surface broken into plate-like blocks separated by long narrow depressions. Scientists do not yet know whether these features represent ancient fossil tectonics or ongoing tectonic activity. One important clue would be evidence of a deep ocean beneath its icy surface, because such an ocean could help drive internal convection.
The rate at which a planet loses heat is critical to tectonic activity. Larger bodies lose heat more slowly and can therefore remain active for longer times. Composition also matters, because it affects whether a body can convect internally. A liquid interior is more likely to support convection than a rigid rocky interior. The abundance of radioactive elements also matters, because their decay produces heat. The Moon, Mercury, and Mars appear either too rigid or too cooled internally to sustain convection and plate tectonics.
Eventually Earth too will cool enough that internal convection will stop. When that happens, earthquake and volcanic activity will cease, no new mountains will form, and major parts of the geologic cycle will come to an end. Exactly how a cooled Earth would change surface conditions, or whether it would remain habitable, is unknown. Fortunately, such changes are not expected for many billions of years.
Over geologic time, plate movements together with erosion and other processes have created some of Earth's most impressive landscapes, including the Himalayas, the Alps, and the Andes. At the same time, plate tectonics is also responsible for destructive natural hazards, including earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and tsunamis.
Most earthquakes and volcanic eruptions do not occur randomly. They are concentrated in specific regions, especially along plate boundaries. One of the most active such regions is the circum-Pacific Ring of Fire, where the Pacific Plate interacts with many surrounding plates.
Because many major population centers are located near active faults, earthquakes have caused enormous human and economic losses. Some popular beliefs about earthquakes are mistaken. A large earthquake will not cause California to break off and fall into the Pacific, nor will the ground open up and swallow people or buildings. The San Andreas Fault is a strike-slip fault, meaning that motion along it is mostly horizontal, and California's relatively low-density continental crust keeps it riding high above the ocean.
The San Andreas Fault separates the Pacific Plate from the North American Plate. The Pacific Plate moves northward relative to the North American Plate. This motion can be seen in offset landforms, geologic structures, and other features along the fault zone. At places such as Crystal Springs Reservoir, erosion of weakened rocks within the fault zone has created long, straight valleys.
Movement along the San Andreas Fault may happen suddenly in earthquakes or gradually through a slow process called creep. Creeping segments produce many small to moderate earthquakes and usually little damage. Locked segments, by contrast, store energy for decades or centuries before releasing it in major earthquakes. The great 1906 San Francisco earthquake ruptured a long previously locked segment of the San Andreas Fault and caused devastating damage.
The release of stress along a fault can be compared to bending a stick until it snaps. As stress builds, the material deforms, and when it finally breaks, stored energy is released suddenly. In earthquakes, this release generates seismic vibrations that spread outward from the rupture point, called the focus. The point on Earth's surface directly above the focus is called the epicenter. In a major earthquake, damaging effects can extend far beyond the immediate area of rupture.
The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake occurred along a segment of the San Andreas Fault that had remained locked since 1906. Although its focus lay in a relatively sparsely populated part of the Santa Cruz Mountains, the earthquake still caused deaths and billions of dollars in damage. Other faults in the San Francisco Bay region, such as the Hayward Fault, may pose comparable or even greater risks because they cut through densely populated urban areas.
The 1994 Northridge earthquake in southern California was one of the costliest natural disasters in United States history. It occurred not on the main San Andreas system, but on a concealed blind thrust fault near Los Angeles. Blind thrust faults do not break the ground surface, making them difficult to identify and map even though they can be highly dangerous.
Not all fault motion is destructive. Along parts of the Calaveras Fault near Hollister, California, steady creep relieves stress continuously. This makes large earthquakes less likely there, but the gradual motion still damages roads, sidewalks, pipes, and other structures that cross the fault.
Mid-plate earthquakes, which occur within the interiors of plates, are much less frequent than boundary earthquakes and are harder to explain. Earthquakes along the Atlantic coast of the United States may be related to the long-term westward movement of the North American Plate away from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, but their precise causes remain uncertain.
Earthquakes in the eastern United States are often felt across much larger areas than similar-magnitude earthquakes in the west. This is because eastern rocks are generally older and less fractured, so they transmit seismic energy more efficiently. Historic eastern earthquakes such as the 1886 Charleston earthquake and the 1811-1812 New Madrid earthquakes were felt over enormous distances.
Scientists understand in general how most earthquakes occur, but predicting exactly when they will happen remains extremely difficult. Since the early 1980s, researchers have closely monitored the Parkfield segment of the San Andreas Fault because it seemed to produce magnitude-6 earthquakes at relatively regular intervals. Although scientists predicted a high probability of such an earthquake before 1993, it did not occur within that expected time window. Even so, the Parkfield experiment greatly improved earthquake monitoring and public awareness.
Volcanic activity is also closely linked to plate tectonics. Most active volcanoes on land are found near convergent boundaries where subduction occurs, especially around the Pacific basin. However, most lava erupted on Earth actually emerges beneath the oceans, especially along spreading centers such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East Pacific Rise.
Subduction-zone volcanoes such as Mount St. Helens and Mount Pinatubo are composite cones and often erupt explosively because their magma is too viscous to let gases escape easily. Pressure builds as the gases expand, and when the pressure is released suddenly, violent eruptions occur.
The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo was one of the largest eruptions of the twentieth century. It sent ash high into the atmosphere and produced pyroclastic flows and mudflows that devastated the surrounding region. The eruption also had global effects. Sulfur dioxide injected into the stratosphere formed sulfuric acid droplets that blocked some sunlight, cooled global temperatures, and contributed to unusually vivid sunsets and sunrises. A similar global cooling effect followed the 1815 eruption of Tambora, which helped produce the year without a summer in 1816.
Explosive eruptions often produce pyroclastic flows, which are fast-moving, extremely hot mixtures of ash, volcanic debris, and gas that race down a volcano's slopes. Such flows caused tens of thousands of deaths during the 1902 eruption of Mount Pelee and devastated villages during the 1982 eruption of El Chichon in Mexico.
Mudflows, also called lahars, are another major volcanic hazard. They are mixtures of water and volcanic debris and can range from watery floods to thick cement-like flows. Lahars can travel rapidly down volcano slopes and bury everything in their paths. In 1985, hot volcanic material from Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia melted snow and ice, generating lahars that buried the city of Armero and killed about 25,000 people.
Major earthquakes along subduction zones can also generate tsunamis. Tsunamis are seismic sea waves, not tidal waves, and can be caused by earthquakes, submarine landslides, or occasionally volcanic eruptions. A large displacement of the seafloor sets huge volumes of water into motion, producing waves that can race across the ocean at jetliner speeds.
In deep ocean water, a tsunami may pass almost unnoticed beneath a ship. But when it reaches shallow coastal water, the wave slows, grows taller, and can surge ashore as a destructive wall of water. Before the main wave arrives, the sea may recede noticeably from the shore, exposing the seafloor for several minutes before the water rushes back inland.
The 1883 eruption of Krakatau produced one of the most destructive eruption-generated tsunamis in history, destroying many coastal villages and killing tens of thousands of people. Because of repeated deadly tsunamis around the Pacific, the International Tsunami Information Center was established in 1965 to issue warnings using data from seismic and tide-gauge networks.
Plate tectonics is not only associated with hazards; it also helps create valuable natural resources. Many important mineral deposits form in tectonically active environments. For example, hydrothermal fluids associated with magmatism can concentrate metals such as copper, gold, silver, lead, and zinc. Fossil fuels also relate to tectonic history because sedimentary basins formed by tectonic processes can accumulate thick deposits of organic material that later become petroleum, natural gas, or coal.
Coal originated from ancient plant material buried in swamps and later compacted beneath sediments. Changes in sea level and tectonic setting helped create the environments in which these deposits formed. Petroleum and natural gas likewise formed from buried organic matter that was transformed by heat and pressure and then migrated into reservoir rocks.
Geothermal energy is another benefit of tectonically active regions. Heat from active or recently active volcanic systems can be used to generate electricity or provide hot water and heating. Iceland and The Geysers geothermal field in California are important examples. Some geothermal fluids also contain economically valuable dissolved minerals.
As population grows and industrialization expands, demand for mineral and energy resources will continue to rise. Many of the easiest-to-reach resources have already been exploited, so attention is increasingly turning to more remote and difficult environments such as the ocean floor, polar regions, and deeper parts of Earth's crust. Meeting future demand without severe environmental harm will be a major challenge.
The long-term benefits of plate tectonics should remind us that Earth is a dynamic and interconnected planet. Understanding plate tectonics has helped unify many branches of earth science and has shown how natural hazards, landscapes, and natural resources are all linked. Humanity must continue learning how to benefit from these long-term tectonic processes while coping with their short-term hazards, especially earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.
