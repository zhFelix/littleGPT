In the early 1960s, the emergence of the theory of plate tectonics started a revolution in the earth sciences. Since then, scientists have verified and refined this theory, and now have a much better understanding of how our planet has been shaped by plate-tectonic processes. We now know that, directly or indirectly, plate tectonics influences nearly all geologic processes, past and present. Indeed, the notion that the entire Earth's surface is continually shifting has profoundly changed the way we view our world.
People benefit from, and are at the mercy of, the forces and consequences of plate tectonics. With little or no warning, an earthquake or volcanic eruption can unleash bursts of energy far more powerful than anything we can generate. While we have no control over plate-tectonic processes, we now have the knowledge to learn from them. The more we know about plate tectonics, the better we can appreciate the grandeur and beauty of the land upon which we live, as well as the occasional violent displays of the Earth's awesome power.
Plate tectonics provides a broad framework for understanding how Earth's surface changes over time. The development of the theory drew on many discoveries and the work of numerous scientists, and its history shows how scientific ideas are tested, refined, and strengthened. Although the general idea of plate tectonics is now widely accepted, many details still challenge researchers and continue to inspire new investigation. In that sense, the earth-science revolution launched by plate tectonics is still unfolding.
In geologic terms, a plate is a large, rigid slab of solid rock. The word tectonics comes from a Greek root meaning "to build." Together, the term plate tectonics refers to the way Earth's surface is constructed from moving plates. The theory of plate tectonics states that Earth's outermost layer is broken into a dozen or more large and small plates that move relative to one another as they ride atop hotter, more mobile material below.
Even before plate tectonics became widely accepted, some scientists had proposed that today's continents were once joined as parts of larger landmasses, often called supercontinents. One of the most important examples is Pangaea, a name meaning "all lands" in Greek. The idea that Pangaea later broke apart figured prominently in the theory of continental drift, which served as an important forerunner to modern plate tectonics.
According to the theory of continental drift, the supercontinent Pangaea began to break apart about 225 to 200 million years ago, eventually fragmenting into the continents we know today.
Plate tectonics is a relatively recent scientific concept, yet it has transformed our understanding of the dynamic planet on which we live. The theory has unified many branches of the earth sciences, from paleontology to seismology, and has helped explain why earthquakes and volcanic eruptions cluster in particular regions and how major mountain ranges such as the Alps and Himalayas formed.
Questions about why Earth is so restless have been asked for centuries. Why does the ground shake violently, why do volcanoes erupt with explosive force, and why do great mountain ranges rise to such heights? Until the 1700s, many Europeans believed that a Biblical Flood played a major role in shaping Earth's surface. This view, known as catastrophism, treated earthly change as the product of sudden destructive events. By the mid-19th century, however, catastrophism gave way to uniformitarianism, a framework associated with James Hutton's idea that "the present is the key to the past." In this view, the geologic forces and processes acting on Earth today, whether gradual or catastrophic, are fundamentally the same kinds of processes that operated in the geologic past.
The idea that continents have not always remained fixed in their present positions was proposed long before the 20th century. As early as 1596, the Dutch map maker Abraham Ortelius suggested that the Americas had been torn away from Europe and Africa. The idea resurfaced in the 19th century, but it was not until 1912 that Alfred Lothar Wegener presented Continental Drift as a full scientific theory. Wegener argued that about 200 million years ago the supercontinent Pangaea began to split apart. Later, Alexander Du Toit proposed that Pangaea first separated into two major landmasses, Laurasia in the north and Gondwanaland in the south, which then broke into the smaller continents known today.
In 1858, geographer Antonio Snider-Pellegrini had already published maps showing how the American and African continents might once have fit together before later separating. Such reconstructions provided visual support for the idea that continents had moved over geologic time.
Wegener's theory was based in part on what appeared to him to be the remarkable fit of the South American and African continents, first noted by Abraham Ortelius three centuries earlier. Wegener was also intrigued by the occurrences of unusual geologic structures and of plant and animal fossils found on the matching coastlines of South America and Africa, which are now widely separated by the Atlantic Ocean. He reasoned that it was physically impossible for most of these organisms to have swum or have been transported across the vast oceans. To him, the presence of identical fossil species along the coastal parts of Africa and South America was the most compelling evidence that the two continents were once joined.
In Wegener's mind, the drifting of continents after the break-up of Pangaea explained not only the matching fossil occurrences but also the evidence of dramatic climate changes on some continents. For example, the discovery of fossils of tropical plants (in the form of coal deposits) in Antarctica led to the conclusion that this frozen land previously must have been situated closer to the equator, in a more temperate climate where lush, swampy vegetation could grow. Other mismatches of geology and climate included distinctive fossil ferns (Glossopteris) discovered in now-polar regions, and the occurrence of glacial deposits in present-day arid Africa, such as the Vaal River valley of South Africa.
The theory of continental drift would become the spark that ignited a new way of viewing the Earth. But at the time Wegener introduced his theory, the scientific community firmly believed the continents and oceans to be permanent features on the Earth's surface. Not surprisingly, his proposal was not well received, even though it seemed to agree with the scientific information available at the time. A fatal weakness in Wegener's theory was that it could not satisfactorily answer the most fundamental question raised by his critics: What kind of forces could be strong enough to move such large masses of solid rock over such great distances? Wegener suggested that the continents simply plowed through the ocean floor, but Harold Jeffreys, a noted English geophysicist, argued correctly that it was physically impossible for a large mass of solid rock to plow through the ocean floor without breaking up.
Undaunted by rejection, Wegener devoted the rest of his life to doggedly pursuing additional evidence to defend his theory. He froze to death in 1930 during an expedition crossing the Greenland ice cap, but the controversy he spawned raged on. However, after his death, new evidence from ocean floor exploration and other studies rekindled interest in Wegener's theory, ultimately leading to the development of the theory of plate tectonics.
Plate tectonics has proven to be as important to the earth sciences as the discovery of the structure of the atom was to physics and chemistry and the theory of evolution was to the life sciences. Even though the theory of plate tectonics is now widely accepted by the scientific community, aspects of the theory are still being debated today. Ironically, one of the chief outstanding questions is the one Wegener failed to resolve: What is the nature of the forces propelling the plates? Scientists also debate how plate tectonics may have operated (if at all) earlier in the Earth's history and whether similar processes operate, or have ever operated, on other planets in our solar system.

Continental drift remained controversial for decades after Wegener's death, and many scientists dismissed it as implausible. Beginning in the 1950s, however, a large body of new evidence revived the debate and helped transform continental drift into the broader theory of plate tectonics. Four developments were especially important: detailed mapping of the ocean floor, recognition of repeated reversals of Earth's magnetic field, the seafloor-spreading hypothesis and the recycling of oceanic crust, and precise evidence that earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated in specific global belts.

About two thirds of Earth's surface lies beneath the oceans, yet for a long time many people assumed the seafloor was flat and featureless. As measurements improved, especially through bathymetric surveys and later echo sounding, scientists discovered that the ocean floor is rugged and dynamic. These studies revealed vast submarine mountain chains, including the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and showed that the mid-ocean ridge system forms a continuous global feature more than 50,000 kilometers long. The thinness of sediment on the ocean floor also suggested that much of the seafloor is geologically young rather than ancient and unchanged.

Another breakthrough came from magnetic measurements of oceanic basalt. Because basalt contains magnetite, it can preserve the direction of Earth's magnetic field at the time the rock cooled. Scientists found that the seafloor contains alternating bands of normal and reversed magnetic polarity arranged in symmetrical stripes on both sides of mid-ocean ridges. This pattern showed that Earth's magnetic field had reversed many times in the past and that new oceanic crust was forming at the ridges and moving outward over time.

These observations led to the seafloor-spreading hypothesis. Scientists proposed that mid-ocean ridges are places where magma rises from below, cools, and creates new oceanic crust. As new crust forms, older crust is pushed away from the ridge crest. Evidence supported this interpretation: rocks nearest the ridge are youngest, rocks farther away are older, and their magnetic patterns record repeated polarity reversals in an orderly way. Later drilling of deep-ocean cores provided strong confirmation, showing that oceanic crust becomes progressively older with distance from the ridge.

Seafloor spreading raised an important question: if new crust is constantly being created, why does Earth not keep growing larger? The answer is that old oceanic crust is also destroyed. Harry Hess and Robert Dietz argued that as crust moves away from spreading ridges, it eventually sinks into deep ocean trenches and returns to the mantle. In this view, ocean basins are continuously recycled. New lithosphere forms at ridges while older lithosphere is consumed at subduction zones, allowing Earth's overall size to remain essentially unchanged.

Earthquake research provided another major line of evidence. With improved seismographs and global seismic networks, scientists found that earthquakes are not distributed randomly. Instead, they cluster along mid-ocean ridges, deep-sea trenches, and other plate boundaries. In some trench regions, earthquake foci define inclined zones descending deep into the Earth, now called Wadati-Benioff zones. These patterns matched the idea that new crust is generated at ridges and older lithosphere sinks back into the mantle at trenches.

Together, ocean-floor mapping, magnetic striping, seafloor spreading, and global earthquake patterns gave scientists a coherent explanation for continental motion and many other geologic processes. Plate tectonics emerged not from one observation alone but from the convergence of many independent discoveries. This is one reason the theory became so powerful: it connected mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes, continents, and ocean basins within a single dynamic picture of Earth.
